## Main purpose / goal
To provide PhD students in economics and finance with practical, highly opinionated advice on how to structure, write, and present their research papers effectively. The author emphasizes that academic papers are essays, urging students to eliminate fluff, organize in a "newspaper" style that reveals the main contribution immediately, and prioritize clear economic identification over complex mathematics or excessive literature reviews.

## Tips / rules / recommendations

### Organization & Structure

#### Figure out the one central and novel contribution of your paper and write it in one concrete paragraph
- **Explanation**: Readers are busy and skim; you must make it easy for them to grasp the basic result without searching for it.
- **Example**: Fama and French (1992) clearly state their main finding about size and book-to-market equity right in the abstract.

- **Explanation**: Put the punchline right up front and fill in the background details later, rather than writing a mystery novel that saves the result for Table 12.

## Key examples or case studies
- **Fama and French 1992**: Used as an example of a perfectly concrete abstract that states the exact variables and results.
- **Fama and French 1996 ("Multifactor explanations")**: Cited as an excellent example of presenting empirical work by showing the stylized facts in the data (expected returns matching betas) rather than just relying on a formal statistical test (the GRS test, which failed).
- **"Bond risk premia" (Cochrane and Piazzesi)**: Mentioned as an example of a "web-appendix gone wild" to store robust checks outside the main paper.

## Warnings / common mistakes to avoid
- Do not start the introduction with broad philosophy (e.g., "Financial economists have long wondered...") or extensive public policy motivation.
- Do not write a "roadmap" paragraph at the end of the introduction (e.g., "Section 2 sets out the model...").
- There should be nothing before the main result that a reader does not need to know in order to understand the main result. Do not include warmup exercises, preliminary estimates, or a travelogue of your failures.
- Do not use footnotes for parenthetical comments; if it is important, put it in the text, and if not, delete it.
- Do not use double adjectives (e.g., "very novel" or "striking results").
- Never use the words "illustrative test" or do empirical work you do not actually believe in.
- Do not include all possible determinants as controls just to increase $R^2$ (e.g., including "industry" in a regression of wages on education, which obscures the mechanism).
- Do not spend the first 30 minutes of a seminar on literature reviews and previews, rushing the main results in the last 10 minutes.

## Conclusions / final advice
Cochrane concludes by reminding readers that economists are primarily writers, not just scientists who "write up" research. He notes that economics and finance papers are essentially essays, and good economists spend at least 50% (and up to 80%) of their project time strictly on writing. He advises students to pay close attention to the writing style of authors they admire and recommends specific books for further improvement, namely William Zinsser's *On Writing Well* and D. McCloskey's *Rhetoric of Economics*.
